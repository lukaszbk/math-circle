Question #5 3 pts Answer: (D) ⚖️ Number Sense, Equations & Grids + Simulation

Justin's Consecutive 4-Digit Numbers

Justin wrote three consecutive 4-digit numbers in a row, and some digits were erased: ___7, _898, 48__. What are the missing digits from left to right?

Core Idea: Glue the clues from the middle and last numbers together—adding 1 to _898 doesn't change its first two digits!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Write ___7, _898, and 48__ side by side on the board and remind the kids that 'consecutive' means counting up by 1 each step.
  2. Compare the 2nd number (_898) and 3rd number (48__): what do you get when you add 1 to a number ending in 898? It ends in 899!
  3. Since the 3rd number starts with 48 and ends in 899, the 3rd number MUST be 4899 — which makes the 2nd number 4898!
  4. Subtract 1 from 4898 to get the 1st number: 4897! Read off the gray blanks from left to right: 489, 4, 99 -> (D).

Ask the Circle

  • What would have happened to the hundreds digit if the middle number had ended in _899 instead of _898?
  • Look at choice (E) 488, 4, 99—what number does 488 put in the first spot, and why isn't 4887 right before 4898?

Common Traps

Choice (E) 488, 4, 99 is a sneaky place-value trap: students subtract 1 from the tens place instead of the ones place (writing 4887 instead of 4897). Choice (C) forgets to add 1 to 98 -> 99 on the third number.

Try at Home

Write three consecutive 4-digit numbers near a rollover (like 4897, 4898, 4899) on scrap paper, cover a few digits with coins, and take turns being the digit detective!

Question #6 3 pts Answer: (C) ⏱️ Measurement, Clocks & Money + Number Sense

Lucia's Three Whole-Dollar Items

Lucia pays 7 dollars in total for 3 items, each costing a different whole-number amount of dollars. How much does the most expensive item cost?

Core Idea: To make the priciest item as expensive as possible, make the other two items as cheap as possible!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Draw 3 price tags on the board: [ $ ? ] + [ $ ? ] + [ $ ? ] = $7, reminding kids every item costs at least $1 and all three tags must be DIFFERENT whole numbers.
  2. Ask: 'What are the two smallest different whole-dollar prices we can put on the first two items?' $1 and $2!
  3. Subtract $1 + $2 = $3 from $7: the third item costs $7 − $3 = $4! Check: $1, $2, and $4 are all different and add up to $7.
  4. Quickly test if $3 could be the most expensive: $1 + $2 + $3 = $6 (too small!). What about $5? Leftover is $2, which would force $1 + $1 (same price, not allowed!). So $4 is the only possible maximum -> (C).

Ask the Circle

  • Why can't two of the items both cost $1 so the third item could cost $5?
  • If Lucia paid $10 for 3 different whole-dollar items instead, what's the most the priciest item could cost?

Common Traps

Choice (D) 5 dollars happens when students split $7 as $5 + $1 + $1, forgetting all three prices must be different! Choice (E) 6 dollars leaves only $1 for the other two items combined.

Try at Home

Hand your child 7 coins (pennies or play dollars) to split into 3 uneven piles with no empty piles and no tied piles—they'll discover 1, 2, and 4 is the only combination!

Question #7 3 pts Answer: (E) 🧊 3D Spatial & Cubes

Felix's Knocked-Off Cube Block

A cat knocked 1 cube block off Felix's 3D block construction, leaving 8 blocks standing and 1 on the ground. Which choice (A)–(E) could be the construction before the block fell?

Core Idea: Map the tower heights on the L-shaped footprint—putting 1 block back adds +1 to JUST ONE stack while every other stack stays identical!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Draw the L-shaped 4-square footprint on the board and count the heights of Felix's remaining towers (front-left → middle → back-corner → front-right): [2, 1, 3, 2] (8 blocks total).
  2. Before the cat knocked 1 block off, three of those heights were the SAME and just one stack had +1 extra block (or a 5th ground spot had 1 block)—for 9 blocks total.
  3. Check the front-right stack (must be ≥ 2): (A) and (B) only have height 1 on the front-right—cross out (A) and (B) instantly!
  4. Check (C) [back corner is only 2 < 3—cross out!] and (D) [front-left is only 1 < 2—cross out!]. Now check (E): heights are [2, 1, 4, 2]—knocking 1 block off the 4-high back tower gives [2, 1, 3, 2] -> (E)!

Ask the Circle

  • How can we tell the back corner stack in Felix's broken tower is 3 blocks tall even though its bottom block is hidden?
  • Why is checking column heights on a floor map way safer than just eyeballing 3D drawings?

Common Traps

Choices (A) and (B) both have 9 blocks total and a tall 4-block back tower, tricking students who count total cubes or only look at the tallest column without checking that the front-right stack shrunk from 2 to 1.

Try at Home

Build the [2, 1, 3, 2] L-shape out of 8 sugar cubes or LEGO bricks on the table, then place a 9th cube on top of the tallest stack so your child can compare it from the same angle as the drawing.

Question #8 3 pts Answer: (B) 🔄 Patterns, Cycles & Step Simulation + Counting & Sets

Gray Tiles Behind the Kangaroo Poster

A Kangaroo poster covers a rectangular section of an alternating gray-and-white checkerboard tile wall. How many gray tiles are hidden behind the poster?

Core Idea: Use the visible border tiles as rulers to measure the hidden rectangle—every 6-tile column is half gray and half white!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Count how many tile COLUMNS wide the poster is by counting the tiles right above its top orange edge: 7 columns wide!
  2. Count how many tile ROWS tall the poster is by counting the tiles right beside its left orange edge: 6 rows tall! So 6 × 7 = 42 tiles are hidden in total.
  3. Notice the wall is a checkerboard where every vertical column alternates Gray, White, Gray, White… Since 6 rows is EVEN, EVERY column of 6 hidden tiles has exactly 3 gray tiles and 3 white tiles!
  4. Multiply 7 columns × 3 gray tiles per column = 21 gray tiles (or add the 6 rows: 3 + 4 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 4 = 21) -> (B).

Ask the Circle

  • Why did every single vertical column behind the poster have the exact same number of gray tiles (3), while the horizontal rows switched between 3 and 4?
  • If the poster covered 5 rows by 7 columns instead (both odd!), how would we decide whether there are more gray tiles or more white tiles?

Common Traps

Choice (C) 25 comes from guessing a 5×5 square or miscounting 7 columns as 8. Choice (D) 30 comes from miscounting the poster as 6×10 ÷ 2, and (A) 15 comes from counting only 5 columns × 3.

Try at Home

Lay an index card over part of a chessboard or checkerboard so its edges line up with the squares, and practice finding how many dark squares are hidden just from the border rows and columns!

Question #13 4 pts Answer: (A) ⚖️ Number Sense, Equations & Grids + Logic

Four Overlapping Sum-10 Rings

Cards 1 through 7 are placed across 4 overlapping rings so that the numbers inside each ring sum to 10; the outer cards are 6 on the left and 3 on the right. Which number is under the central question mark?

Core Idea: Start at the two end rings that hold only TWO cards each—they unlock the overlaps right away!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Point to the leftmost ring: it holds ONLY two cards—6 and its neighbor! Since every ring sums to 10, that neighbor card MUST be 10 − 6 = 4!
  2. Point to the rightmost ring: it holds ONLY two cards—its neighbor and 3! So that neighbor card MUST be 10 − 3 = 7!
  3. Cross off {3, 4, 6, 7} from {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7}. Only {1, 2, 5} remain for the middle three cards [left-mid, ?, right-mid]!
  4. Check the two middle 3-card rings: left-middle ring needs 4 + [left-mid] + [?] = 10 (so [left-mid] + [?] = 6 -> must be {5, 1}!), and right-middle ring needs [?] + [right-mid] + 7 = 10 (so [?] + [right-mid] = 3 -> must be {1, 2}!). The card in BOTH pairs is 1 -> (A)!

Ask the Circle

  • Bonus superpower check: 1+2+3+4+5+6+7 = 28, while 4 rings × 10 = 40! Why is 40 bigger than 28 by 12, and which three cards got counted twice?
  • Once we know the three double-counted overlap cards are 4, ?, and 7, and they must add to 12, what is '?' instantly?

Common Traps

Choice (C) 4 and Choice (E) 7 are the two other overlap cards! Students who misread which ring holds which card or mix up the middle slots often pick (B) 2 or (D) 5 (the two neighbors of '?').

Try at Home

Set out 7 playing cards Ace (1) through 7 and four loops of string or yarn on the table—let your child solve from the outer 2-card loops inward!

Question #14 4 pts Answer: (B) 🔢 Systematic Counting & Sets + 2D Shapes

Lucas's Puzzle-Piece Caterpillars

Lucas connects a head, a banana tail, and 1, 2, or 3 middle fruit puzzle pieces without flipping pieces over. How many different valid caterpillars can he build?

Core Idea: Label each connector Round (R) or Square-T (T)—the Cherry piece is the ONLY bridge from Round to Square-T!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Inspect the 5 pieces from left to right on the board: Head ends with a Round socket (-R), and Banana Tail starts with a T-tab (+T)! Because Lucas CANNOT flip pieces over, left stays left and right stays right.
  2. Label the 3 middle pieces by (left tab → right socket): Apple is (T → T), Cherry is (R → T), and Grapes is (R → R).
  3. Notice: to get from Head's Round socket to Tail's T-tab, EVERY caterpillar MUST use the Cherry piece (R → T) as its bridge!
  4. Count choices before and after Cherry: before Cherry we can include Grapes (R → R) or skip it (2 ways); after Cherry we can include Apple (T → T) or skip it (2 ways). That gives 2 × 2 = 4 caterpillars: [C], [G-C], [C-A], and [G-C-A] -> (B)!

Ask the Circle

  • Why can't Apple EVER go right after the Head, even though both are puzzle pieces?
  • Why can't Lucas build a caterpillar using only Grapes and Apple without Cherry?

Common Traps

Choice (D) 6 or (E) 7 happens when students count permutations of 3 pieces without checking Round vs. T-shaped connectors, or accidentally flip pieces upside down so tabs and sockets swap sides.

Try at Home

Cut 5 index cards with simple round vs. square notches on their left and right edges matching the picture—trying to snap them together without flipping makes the 4 valid caterpillars click immediately!

Question #15 4 pts Answer: (B) 🧊 3D Spatial & Cubes + Logic

John's Flipped Two-Sided Number Sheet

John writes 1–4 on the dark front of a 2×2 sheet, flips it horizontally to write 5–8 on the light back, cuts out the 4 cards, and lays them as [?] [5] [?] [6]. What is the sum of the two '?' numbers?

Core Idea: When you flip a page like a book, Top-Left swaps with Top-Right—pair up each card's front and back before looking at the row!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Hold up a folded or marked sheet of paper and flip it left-to-right following the curved arrow: Top-Left (1) lands at Top-Right (7), and Top-Right (2) lands at Top-Left (6)!
  2. Check the bottom row the same way: Bottom-Left (4) lands at Bottom-Right (5), and Bottom-Right (3) lands at Bottom-Left (8). Write the 4 two-sided cards on the board: {1,7}, {2,6}, {3,8}, {4,5}.
  3. Look at the 4 cards in the row [?] [5] [?] [6]: one card is already showing 5 (that uses up card {4,5}!) and one card is showing 6 (that uses up card {2,6}!).
  4. The two dark [?] cards MUST be the remaining two cards {1,7} and {3,8} with their dark sides up—showing 1 and 3! Their sum is 1 + 3 = 4 -> (B).

Ask the Circle

  • Why didn't we even need to know WHICH question mark is 1 and which one is 3?
  • What numbers are hiding on the backs of the '5' card and the '6' card?

Common Traps

Choice (D) 6 is the 'no-flip' trap! Students who overlay the two grids without flipping pair 1 with 6, 2 with 7, 4 with 8, and 3 with 5—crossing out 1 and 3 and adding 2 + 4 = 6!

Try at Home

Fold or rule a scrap of paper into 4 boxes, write 1, 2, 4, 3 on the front, flip it left-to-right to write 6, 7, 8, 5 on the back, and cut the 4 cards out to reveal the pairs!

Question #16 4 pts Answer: (D) ⏱️ Measurement, Clocks & Money + 2D Shapes

Rectangles and Squares Floor Tiling

A floor pattern uses 23 cm × 11 cm light rectangles and dark squares. Using how the tiles line up, find the side length of one dark square tile.

Core Idea: Find two tile rows that start and end along the EXACT same vertical lines—one long 23 cm side matches a 11 cm short side plus TWO dark squares!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Highlight ONE complete horizontal light rectangle in the middle of the floor and label its long horizontal edge 23 cm.
  2. Look directly above (or below) that 23 cm edge from its left end to its right end: sitting right on top of it are 1 dark square + the short end of 1 vertical rectangle (11 cm) + 1 dark square!
  3. Write the matching-length equation on the board: Square + 11 cm + Square = 23 cm!
  4. Subtract 11 cm from 23 cm: TWO dark squares = 12 cm, so ONE dark square = 12 ÷ 2 = 6 cm -> (D).

Ask the Circle

  • Can you spot the exact same 'Square + 11 + Square = 23' match going UP-AND-DOWN along a vertical rectangle?
  • Why would subtracting 23 − 11 = 12 without dividing by 2 give too big an answer?

Common Traps

Students who think ONE dark square plus 11 cm equals 23 cm get 12 (not an option) and then guess (C) 5 cm or (E) 7 cm by eye, or compute 23 − 3 × 6 mistakes instead of tracing one clean 23 cm edge.

Try at Home

Cut a few 23 mm × 11 mm paper strips (or LEGO 4×2 bricks alongside 1×1 studs) so your child can line up the corners and see two small squares + one short side equal one long side.

Question #21 5 pts Answer: (D) 🔢 Systematic Counting & Sets + Logic

Simultaneous Frog Jumps on a 3×3 Grid

Six frogs sit on a 3×3 grid with 2 frogs in every row and column (empty cells at top-middle, middle-right, bottom-left). Two frogs simultaneously jump into neighboring empty cells while keeping 2 frogs per row and column. In how many ways can they do this?

Core Idea: To keep 2 frogs in every row and column, the 2 jumping frogs must trade places across the SAME two columns (left/right) or the SAME two rows (up/down)!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Draw the 3×3 grid on the board with the 6 frogs and mark the 3 empty squares E at (row 1, col 2), (row 2, col 3), and (row 3, col 1).
  2. Ask: 'If one frog jumps sideways from col 1 into col 2, col 1 loses a frog and col 2 gains one! How can the second frog fix that?' It MUST jump sideways the opposite way, from col 2 into col 1! (And you can never mix a sideways jump with an up/down jump!)
  3. Count SIDEWAYS swaps: (1) Col 1 ↔ Col 2: top-left jumps right to (1,2) AND bottom-middle jumps left to (3,1) [1 way]. (2) Col 2 ↔ Col 3: center jumps right to (2,3) AND top-right jumps left to (1,2) [1 way]. (Col 1 & Col 3 aren't neighbors!)
  4. Count UP/DOWN swaps: (3) Row 1 ↔ Row 2: top-right jumps down to (2,3) AND center jumps up to (1,2) [1 way]. (4) Row 2 ↔ Row 3: middle-left jumps down to (3,1) AND bottom-right jumps up to (2,3) [1 way]. Total = 2 + 2 = 4 ways -> (D)!

Ask the Circle

  • Look closely at Ways #2 and #3 (both fill the top-middle and middle-right squares!): why are they TWO different ways for the frogs to jump?
  • Why can't one frog jump sideways while the other frog jumps up or down?

Common Traps

Choice (C) 3 is the classic trap! Students count how many final 3×3 pictures exist (3 grids, since one of the 3 empty squares stays empty) and miss that the top-right 2×2 corner lets frogs reach the same picture in 2 different ways (both jumping sideways OR both jumping up/down)!

Try at Home

Draw a 3×3 grid and place 6 coins of DIFFERENT kinds (penny, nickel, dime, quarter, button, paperclip) on the frog squares—having distinct tokens makes it easy to see that Ways #2 and #3 move each frog to a different square!

Question #22 5 pts Answer: (C) 🧩 Deductive Logic & Constraints + Counting & Sets

Honeycomb Neighbor Clues

In a 9-cell honeycomb, the number inside each cell tells how many of its side-sharing neighbor cells contain honey (a cell with a number may also contain honey itself). How many cells contain honey?

Core Idea: Hunt for edge cells whose clue number EQUALS their total number of neighbors—every neighbor of those cells MUST have honey!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Remind kids of the golden rule: a cell's number counts its SIDE-SHARING NEIGHBORS with honey (NOT itself!), and ANY cell can hold honey.
  2. Spot the three '100% full' border clues right away: (1) Top '3' has ONLY 3 neighbors -> mark all 3 (upper-left '1', center '4', upper-right '3') with HONEY! (2) Far-right '2' has ONLY 2 neighbors -> mark both (upper-right '3', lower-right '4') with HONEY! (3) Lower-right '4' has ONLY 4 neighbors -> mark all 4 (center '4', bottom '2', upper-right '3', far-right '2') with HONEY!
  3. That already locks in 6 honey cells! Now check the remaining 3 cells on the left/top using the '1' clues: far-left '1' already touches upper-left '1' (honey!), so lower-left '3' has NO honey.
  4. Upper-left '1' already touches center '4' (honey!), so its other neighbors (far-left '1' and top '3') have NO honey! Double-check center '4' (touches 4 honey cells ✓) and count: 6 honey cells -> (C).

Ask the Circle

  • Why were the top '3', lower-right '4', and far-right '2' much better starting points than the center '4'?
  • Does a cell's own number change if we put honey inside that cell itself?

Common Traps

Students who think a cell with honey cannot have a clue number get stuck immediately, while students who forget to count whether lower-right '4' itself has honey (from far-right '2'!) come up short with (B) 5.

Try at Home

Sketch the 9 hexagons on paper and use yellow coins for 'honey' and 'X' tokens for 'empty'—starting from border cells where clue = neighbor count feels just like solving Minesweeper!

Question #23 5 pts Answer: (E) 🧩 Deductive Logic & Constraints + Counting & Sets

Three Girls and the Cookie Tray

One after another (in unknown order), three girls take all available heart cookies, all available white cookies, and all available large cookies from a 16-cookie tray. Their totals are 3, 6, and 7 cookies. Which set of cookies did one girl take?

Core Idea: Count how many Hearts, Whites, and Larges are on the full tray first—only someone whose full count is 3, 6, or 7 could go FIRST!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Sort the 16 cookies on the tray on the board: 2 Large White Circles, 4 Large Brown Hearts, 1 Large White Heart, 1 Small White Heart, 5 Small Brown Hearts, 3 Small White Circles.
  2. Check full-tray totals to see who could go FIRST (must take 3, 6, or 7): Hearts = 11 (NO!), White = 7 (maybe!), Large = 7 (maybe!).
  3. Test if WHITE goes 1st (takes 7 whites, leaving ONLY 4 Large Brown Hearts + 5 Small Brown Hearts): then Large next would take 4 (not 3 or 6!), and Heart next would take 9 (not 3 or 6!). So White CANNOT go 1st!
  4. Therefore LARGE goes 1st (takes 7 large cookies, leaving 1 Small White Heart, 5 Small Brown Hearts, 3 Small White Circles)! Next, HEART goes 2nd (takes 1 + 5 = 6 small hearts), leaving WHITE to go 3rd and take the 3 Small White Circles -> (E)!

Ask the Circle

  • Why is Choice (B) (all 7 white cookies) such a clever trap if you stop after checking the first girl?
  • Why couldn't the White-cookie girl go 2nd right after the Large-cookie girl?

Common Traps

Choice (B) is the biggest trap on the test: there really ARE 7 white cookies on the tray, so students who see 7 white cookies in (B) pick it immediately without checking that the remaining 9 brown hearts can't split into 6 and 3!

Try at Home

Draw or cut out the 16 cookies on slips of paper and let your child act out different turn orders for the 'Large', 'Heart', and 'White' grabbers to see why only Large → Heart → White gives 7, 6, and 3!

Question #24 5 pts Answer: (D) 🧊 3D Spatial & Cubes + Counting & Sets

Minimum White Blocks for the Large Cube

Each small cube in a 2×2×2 large cube is built from either 4 white wedge blocks or 1 white + 1 red block. Given the visible red, solid-white, and X-seamed white faces, what is the smallest number of white blocks needed?

Core Idea: Every single small cube needs AT LEAST 1 white block—and only the cubes showing an all-white 'X' face are forced to use all 4 white blocks!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. First ask: 'How many small cubes are in a 2×2×2 large cube?' 8 small cubes (7 visible + 1 hidden in the back-bottom-left corner)—and EVEN a mostly-red cube uses 1 white + 1 red block, so we start with at least 1 white block in all 8 cubes!
  2. Inspect what the two recipes look like on the outside: a '4-white' cube has 4 smooth white square faces and 2 all-white 'X' faces; a '1-white + 1-red' cube STILL has 1 smooth white square face (plus 3 smooth red faces and 2 V-shaped faces we can hide inside or in back)!
  3. Spot the small cubes that MUST use 4 white blocks: the top-front-right cube (shows an all-white 'X' on top + 2 white sides) and the bottom-front-left cube (shows an all-white 'X' on front)—that's 2 cubes × 4 = 8 white blocks!
  4. Verify every other small cube (the 3 cubes showing just ONE smooth white face, the 2 showing red faces, and the 1 hidden cube = 6 cubes) can be built from 1 white + 1 red (6 × 1 = 6 white blocks). Total = 8 + 6 = 14 -> (D)!

Ask the Circle

  • Why doesn't a small cube showing ONE smooth white square face need 4 white blocks?
  • Look at the wrong choices 11, 13, and 23—what did someone forget if they got 13 (one less than 14)?

Common Traps

Every wrong answer tells a story: (E) 23 assumes all 5 cubes with any white face need 4 white blocks (5×4 + 3×1); (C) 13 forgets the 8th hidden cube in back (2×4 + 5×1); (B) 11 thinks red/hidden cubes use 0 white blocks (2×4 + 3×1).

Try at Home

Cut a square sticky note along both diagonals into 4 triangles to model the 4 wedges of a small cube's top face—seeing that 1 white wedge already gives 1 full white side edge makes the '1 white + 1 red' trick click!
