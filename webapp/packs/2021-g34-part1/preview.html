Question #1 3 pts Answer: (C) 🧊 3D Spatial & Cubes

Erik's Four Bricks

Erik has 1 white brick and 3 gray bricks (each made of 2 cubes). Which 2×2×2 cube can he build with his 4 bricks?

Core Idea: Count the white bricks first—Erik only has ONE white brick (just 2 small cubes!), not two!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Point to Erik's 4 bricks at the top: each brick is 2 unit cubes long (2×1×1), so 4 bricks make a 2×2×2 cube (8 unit cubes total).
  2. Check the colors: Erik has ONLY 1 white brick (2 unit cubes) and 3 gray bricks (6 unit cubes).
  3. Check how many white unit cubes appear in each choice! In (A), (B), (D), and (E), you can see at least 3 or 4 white unit cubes (or two separate white bricks)!
  4. Verify (C): it has ONE vertical white brick on the front-left (2 cubes) and three gray bricks (front-right vertical, back-top horizontal, back-bottom horizontal) -> (C).

Ask the Circle

  • How many little unit cubes are in ONE brick, and how many white squares can we possibly see from 1 white brick?
  • Why is choice (A) impossible even though its front looks like one white brick on the bottom and one gray brick on top?

Common Traps

Choice (A) tricks kids who glance only at the front face (2 gray on top, 2 white on bottom) and miss the white half-brick sticking up on the right side (making 2 white bricks!). Choices (B), (D), and (E) all contain 2 white bricks.

Try at Home

Build a 2×2×2 cube on the table with 8 LEGO bricks or sugar cubes (2 white, 6 gray) so your child can spin the model and see why only 1 white brick can ever show.

Question #2 3 pts Answer: (C) 📐 2D Shapes, Symmetry & Paths

Fish on a Tangled Line

Ten colorful fish are tied along a looped fishing line with a ring at one end. How many fish will have their heads pointing toward the ring when the line is straightened?

Core Idea: Walk your finger along the string from the ring to the free end: every fish you enter nose-first points toward the ring!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Explain why straightening the line never flips a fish on the rope: a fish pointing toward the ring along the path STILL points toward the ring when pulled straight!
  2. Put your finger on the ring 'O' and trace slowly along the rope, numbering each fish 1 to 10 and shouting 'HEAD!' (nose-first) or 'TAIL!' (tail-first).
  3. Trace #1–#5: #1 green (HEAD ✓), #2 pink (TAIL ✗), #3 yellow (HEAD ✓), #4 pink (TAIL ✗), #5 blue (TAIL ✗).
  4. Trace #6–#10 carefully through the loop: #6 tilted blue (HEAD ✓), #7 top yellow (HEAD ✓), #8 top-left green (HEAD ✓), #9 upper blue (TAIL ✗), #10 far-right pink (HEAD ✓). Count the checkmarks: 6 fish -> (C)!

Ask the Circle

  • At a rope crossing, how can we tell which strand continues our path instead of jumping onto a crossing strand?
  • If 6 fish out of 10 point toward the ring, how many point away from the ring (and how can we use that to double-check)?

Common Traps

Choices (B) 5 and (D) 7 happen when kids misread the tilted middle blue fish (#6) or accidentally turn onto the wrong strand where two loops cross. Counting the 4 away-facing fish gives (A) 3 or (B) 5 if one is miscounted.

Try at Home

Lay a shoelace or piece of yarn on the kitchen table with paper arrows taped on as fish, trace from the ring end with one finger, then pull the yarn straight to prove the arrows never flip.

Question #3 3 pts Answer: (B) 📐 2D Shapes, Symmetry & Paths + Number Sense

Jigsaw Puzzle Sum

Four jigsaw pieces showing '3', '2', '1', and '+' snap together into a rectangle with a calculation. What is the result of the calculation?

Core Idea: Put the flat outer border pieces on the far left and far right first—then snap the tabs and holes together!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Look at the outer straight edges: only the '1' piece has a FLAT left side, so '1' MUST go on the far left! And only the '2' piece has a FLAT right side, so '2' MUST go on the far right!
  2. Check the middle connection from '1': '1' sticks out a tab on its right, so the next piece must have a hole on its left ('3' or '2', and '2' is already on the right!). So '3' goes next to '1'!
  3. Check the '+' piece between '3' and '2': '+' has tabs sticking out on BOTH sides, which snap neatly into '3''s right hole and '2''s left hole!
  4. Read the finished rectangle from left to right: 13 + 2 = 15 -> (B).

Ask the Circle

  • Can we flip the puzzle pieces upside down, or do the numbers '3', '2', '1' tell us which side is top?
  • Which edge clues immediately lock '1' and '2' into the ends without even testing the middle?

Common Traps

Choice (D) 24 (from 21 + 3 or 23 + 1) and Choice (E) 33 (from 32 + 1 or 31 + 2) come from rearranging the digits freely without checking the flat borders and jigsaw tabs/holes. Choice (A) 6 just adds 3 + 2 + 1.

Try at Home

Cut 4 small paper puzzle pieces with matching tabs and flat outer edges so your child can physically snap the left and right border pieces into place before reading the sum.

Question #4 3 pts Answer: (B) 📐 2D Shapes, Symmetry & Paths

Alaya's Sun Rays

Alaya drew a smiling sun with white, light-gray, and dark-gray rays of different lengths. Which answer choice shows an exact piece of her sun?

Core Idea: Notice that color matches length (Dark = Long, Light = Medium, White = Short), and always walk CLOCKWISE around the sun—never flip like a mirror!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Spot the secret code on Alaya's sun: Dark gray rays are LONG, Light gray rays are MEDIUM, and White rays are SHORT!
  2. Show how spinning a piece to the top of the circle keeps the left-to-right order the same as walking CLOCKWISE around the sun (like a clock hand moving 8 → 9 → 10 → 11).
  3. Check the left side of Alaya's sun from 8 o'clock to 10 o'clock going clockwise: Dark (Long) → Light gray (Medium) → White (Short) → Dark (Long)!
  4. Match against the choices: only (B) has Dark-Long, Light-Medium, White-Short, Dark-Long in order -> (B)!

Ask the Circle

  • Why does spinning a piece of the sun keep the same clockwise order, while reflecting it in a mirror reverses the order?
  • Find where choice (E) came from on Alaya's sun—why is (E) backwards?

Common Traps

Choice (E) (White, White, Dark, White) is the classic mirror trap: it reads the rays from 11:30 back down to 9:30 counterclockwise instead of clockwise! Choices (A), (C), and (D) scramble ray colors or lengths.

Try at Home

Draw a ring of colored rays around a paper plate and spin the plate on the table (without flipping it over) to help your child see why clockwise order stays fixed under rotation.

Question #9 4 pts Answer: (A) 🔄 Patterns, Cycles & Step Simulation + Logic

Nisa's Fruit Card Swap

Nisa wants to swap just 2 cards in a set of 5 fruit cards so that all cards with the same fruit are next to each other. For which set is this NOT possible?

Core Idea: Prove four choices work with ONE real swap—and notice in (A) that BOTH pairs of matching fruits are broken apart!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Use letters A (Apple), C (Cherry), G (Grapes) on the whiteboard and remember: Nisa only gets ONE swap of 2 cards!
  2. Find the winning 1-swap move in the four possible sets: (B) A C A G A → swap #2 (C) & #5 (A) to get A A A G C ✓; (C) G A A G C → swap #1 (G) & #3 (A) to get A A G G C ✓.
  3. Check (D) and (E): (D) C C A G C → swap #3 (A) & #5 (C) to get C C C G A ✓; (E) A G C G C → swap #3 (C) & #4 (G) to get A G G C C ✓.
  4. Now test (A) A C G A G: the two Apples (1, 4) are separated AND the two Grapes (3, 5) are separated! You'd need to fix BOTH pairs, which takes at least 2 swaps—so 1 swap can NEVER fix (A) -> (A)!

Ask the Circle

  • In choice (B), there are three apples at spots 1, 3, and 5—how can a single swap possibly group all three apples together?
  • Why does having TWO split-up pairs (apples AND grapes) in choice (A) make a single swap impossible?

Common Traps

Choice (B) looks suspicious at first glance because 3 apples are spread out (A, C, A, G, A), tricking students who forget they can swap the single Cherry at #2 with the end Apple at #5!

Try at Home

Set out 5 coins or playing cards (for example, 2 pennies, 2 nickels, and 1 dime) in each pattern and challenge your child to group matching coins together using only ONE two-card swap.

Question #10 4 pts Answer: (E) 🧩 Deductive Logic & Constraints

Sofie's Five Shapes

Sofie picks 5 different shapes, taking 1 shape from each of the 5 containers. Which shape must she pick from container 4?

Core Idea: Start with the pickiest container—the one that holds only ONE shape—and cross that shape out everywhere else!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Scan containers 1 to 5 to find who has the fewest choices: Container 3 only has a CIRCLE! Lock in Container 3 = Circle.
  2. Cross out Circle from every other container. Now look at Container 1 (Star, Circle): with Circle gone, Container 1 MUST give STAR!
  3. Cross out Star everywhere else. Now look at Container 5 (Pentagon, Star): with Star gone, Container 5 MUST give PENTAGON!
  4. Cross out Pentagon everywhere else and check Container 4 (Star, Pentagon, Circle, Diamond): Star, Pentagon, and Circle are all taken, leaving ONLY DIAMOND -> (E)! (And Container 2 gets Triangle.)

Ask the Circle

  • Why was Container 2 (with all 5 shapes) the worst place to start, and Container 3 the best place?
  • Which shape did Container 2 end up giving Sofie?

Common Traps

Kids who look at Container 4 right away see 4 shapes and feel stuck, or pick (D) Triangle because Triangle only appears in Container 2 (forgetting Triangle isn't even inside Container 4!).

Try at Home

Draw 5 bowls on scratch paper and practice the 'start with the pickiest bowl' strategy—crossing out each locked-in shape from all the other bowls step by step.

Question #11 4 pts Answer: (E) 🧊 3D Spatial & Cubes

Missing Gray Cubes

18 unit cubes (white, gray, black) form a 3×3×2 block. Given the white and black pieces on the right, which shape is the gray piece?

Core Idea: Split the 18-cube block into two flat 3×3 floors (top floor and bottom floor)—the top floor is already 100% full!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Count the 3×3×2 block by layers: 9 cubes on the top floor and 9 cubes on the bottom floor (18 total).
  2. Count the top floor first: the black U-piece has 7 cubes, and the white piece has 2 cubes on the top floor (look closely: 7 + 2 = 9!). So NO gray cubes are on the top floor!
  3. Now check the bottom floor: the black piece uses 0 bottom cubes, and the white piece uses ONLY 2 bottom cubes (front-middle and front-right—notice there's empty air under the back-top white cube!).
  4. Subtract those 2 front-right white cubes from the 3×3 bottom floor: you have 3 along the back, 3 across the middle, and 1 on the front-left (7 gray cubes) -> (E)!

Ask the Circle

  • How can we tell from the 18-cube block that the white piece does NOT have a bottom cube on the back-middle?
  • Why does the visible gray side on the right wall of the 3×3×2 block immediately rule out choice (C)?

Common Traps

Choice (C) is a sneaky trap: it misses the middle-right gray cube (even though you can see two gray squares on the right wall of the big block!). Choice (D) misses the middle-left cube, and (A) copies the black U-piece.

Try at Home

Stack 18 building blocks or dice into two flat 3×3 layers (top floor and bottom floor) and lift off the top layer first so your child can see which spots are left on the bottom floor.

Question #12 4 pts Answer: (C) 🔄 Patterns, Cycles & Step Simulation + Number Sense

Colliding Number Balls

Five numbered balls (10→, 9→, ←3, ←7, ←20) move as shown; when opposite balls collide, the bigger ball swallows the smaller one and keeps moving in its direction. What is the final ball?

Core Idea: Always collide neighbors moving TOWARD each other first—and watch who wins when 19→ crashes into ←20!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Write all 5 balls with arrows on the board: [10→] [9→] [←3] [←7] [←20]. Notice [9→] and [←3] are heading straight toward each other!
  2. Collision 1: [9→] swallows [←3] (9 > 3) to become [12→]. Line is now: [10→] [12→] [←7] [←20].
  3. Collision 2: [12→] swallows [←7] (12 > 7) to become [19→]. Now ONLY [19→] and [←20] face each other!
  4. Collision 3 & 4: [←20] is BIGGER than [19→] (20 > 19!), so it flips the winner's direction to LEFT: [←39]! Finally [←39] swallows [10→] (39 > 10) to make [←49] -> (C)!

Ask the Circle

  • Why don't [10→] and [9→] collide at the very start?
  • Why did the final arrow point LEFT instead of RIGHT, even though the first two collisions went RIGHT?

Common Traps

Choices (A) and (D) say 50, catching kids who mis-add 10 + 9 + 3 + 7 + 20 = 49 as 50! Choice (E) →49 gets the right sum 49 but forgets that 20 > 19 turns the combined ball leftward.

Try at Home

Write the five numbers with arrows on sticky notes and slide only arrows pointing toward each other together one collision at a time, replacing the pair with a new sticky note.

Question #17 5 pts Answer: (D) 🧩 Deductive Logic & Constraints

Eva's Five Stickers

Eva puts 5 stickers (triangle, circle, star, flower, apple) on squares 1–5: star is not on 5, apple is on 1, and flower is next to both circle and triangle. Which square gets the flower?

Core Idea: Glue [Circle – Flower – Triangle] into one 3-square train with Flower in the middle, then check where Star fits!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Draw 5 boxes labeled 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 on the board and immediately put APPLE in box 1.
  2. Look at the clue 'Flower is next to Circle AND Triangle': that means Flower MUST sit in the middle of a 3-box block [Circle, Flower, Triangle] (or [Triangle, Flower, Circle]).
  3. There are only 4 empty boxes left (2, 3, 4, 5), so the 3-box train can ONLY go on {2, 3, 4} or {3, 4, 5}!
  4. Try {2, 3, 4}: that forces STAR onto box 5—which breaks 'Star is NOT on 5'! So the train MUST go on {3, 4, 5} (with Star on box 2), putting FLOWER in the middle on box 4 -> (D)!

Ask the Circle

  • Why can't Flower sit on square 2 or square 5 if it has to touch BOTH Circle and Triangle?
  • How many different complete sticker arrangements satisfy all the clues—and is Flower on square 4 in all of them?

Common Traps

Choice (C) 3 is the top trap: students drop [Circle, Flower, Triangle] right after Apple on squares 2, 3, 4 and forget to check the leftover rule that Star cannot go on square 5.

Try at Home

Draw 5 numbered parking spots on paper and use 5 small toys as the stickers—tape three toys into a 3-car train with Flower in the middle so your child can slide it into spots 2–4 vs. 3–5.

Question #18 5 pts Answer: (E) ⚖️ Number Sense, Equations & Grids

Upside-Down Number Cards

Seven cards A–G each have a number on top and an upside-down number on bottom. Which single card must the teacher turn upside down so both rows have the same sum?

Core Idea: Find the target half-sum first—and remember that flipping ONE card adds to one row AND subtracts the same amount from the other row!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Carefully read each card's (top, bottom) pair right-side up: A(7,4), B(5,3), C(4,5), D(2,5), E(8,7), F(3,7), G(2,4).
  2. Add the Top row: 7 + 5 + 4 + 2 + 8 + 3 + 2 = 31. Add the Bottom row: 4 + 3 + 5 + 5 + 7 + 7 + 4 = 35.
  3. Notice the gap is 35 − 31 = 4, and the grand total is 31 + 35 = 66, so BOTH rows must equal 66 ÷ 2 = 33!
  4. To lift Top from 31 to 33 (+2) and drop Bottom from 35 to 33 (−2), flip a card whose bottom is 2 BIGGER than its top: Card G has 2 on top and 4 on bottom (4 − 2 = 2) -> (E) G!

Ask the Circle

  • Why does flipping a card with a difference of 4 (like Card F: 3 and 7) change the gap between rows by 8 instead of 4?
  • Check our upside-down reading: why is the bottom of Card C a '5' and not a '2' (compare it with the '5' on top of Card B)?

Common Traps

Choice (C) Card D (2 and 5, difference 3) and Choice (D) Card F (3 and 7, difference 4) catch kids who look for a card whose difference equals the whole gap 4 (forgetting flipping affects BOTH rows!) or misread upside-down 5s and 2s.

Try at Home

Write the 7 top/bottom number pairs on folded index cards on the table and have your child flip one card at a time to watch both row totals change at once.

Question #19 5 pts Answer: (D) ⚖️ Number Sense, Equations & Grids + Logic

Neighboring Sum Strip

The numbers 1 to 9 are placed in 9 squares so each bracket shows the sum of two neighbors. Which number belongs in the shaded 5th square?

Core Idea: Attack the tiny sum '3' first—the ONLY two different digits from 1 to 9 that add up to 3 are 1 and 2!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Spot the bottom bracket labeled '3' covering squares 3 and 4: using digits 1–9 once each, ONLY 1 and 2 add up to 3!
  2. Test which order goes in squares 3 and 4 by looking left: could square 3 = 2 and square 4 = 1? Then square 2 = 7 − 2 = 5, which forces square 1 = 15 − 5 = 10 (impossible—10 isn't in 1..9!).
  3. So square 3 MUST be 1 and square 4 MUST be 2! Double-check going left: square 2 = 7 − 1 = 6, and square 1 = 15 − 6 = 9 (all valid!).
  4. Now step right from square 4 (= 2) using the top bracket '9' over squares 4 and 5: 2 + shaded = 9, so the shaded square is 7 -> (D)!

Ask the Circle

  • Why couldn't we start from a big sum like '15' (which could be 6+9 or 7+8), whereas '3' locked in the exact two digits right away?
  • Bonus challenge: can you fill in all 9 squares from left to right? (Hint: 1+2+...+9 = 45, and the 4 bottom brackets add to 41!)

Common Traps

Putting 2 and 1 in the wrong order gives 9 − 1 = 8 (E) without checking the left wall (which would force 10 in square 1). Guessing from the 15-bracket under squares 5–6 leads to picking 6 (C) or 8 (E) without pinning down squares 3–4.

Try at Home

Write digits 1–9 on small paper tiles beside a 9-box strip so your child can physically place 1 and 2 on the '3' bracket first and test both orders against the left neighbors.

Question #20 5 pts Answer: (A) 🔢 Systematic Counting & Sets + Number Sense

Mia's Dartboard Balloons

Mia throws darts at balloons worth 3, 9, 13, 14, and 18 points and scores 30 points total. Which balloon does Mia definitely hit?

Core Idea: When a question says 'definitely hit', find ALL ways to make 30—the answer is the balloon that shows up in EVERY solution!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. List the balloon values on the board: 3, 9, 13, 14, 18. We need subsets that add up to 30.
  2. Try using the biggest balloon, 18: 30 − 18 = 12 left. From {3, 9, 13, 14}, only 9 + 3 = 12 works! So Way #1 = {3, 9, 18}.
  3. Try WITHOUT 18 (using {3, 9, 13, 14}): check 14 + 13 = 27, which needs 30 − 27 = 3 more! So Way #2 = {3, 13, 14}. (Any combo without 13 or without 14 is way below 30: 14+9+3 = 26.)
  4. Compare Way #1 {3, 9, 18} and Way #2 {3, 13, 14}: which single balloon is in BOTH winning groups? Only 3 -> (A)!

Ask the Circle

  • If Mia hit 18, 9, and 3, why isn't (E) 18 the right answer?
  • Even if balloons didn't pop so Mia could hit the same number twice, could she ever reach 30 without hitting 3? (Check multiples of 3!)

Common Traps

Students who find ONE way to make 30 (like 18 + 9 + 3 = 30) and stop right away often pick (E) 18 or (B) 9, missing that 'definitely hit' means it must be part of EVERY possible way to score 30!

Try at Home

Write 3, 9, 13, 14, and 18 on index cards and challenge your child to find EVERY different hand of cards summing to 30, then circle the card that appears in every winning hand.
