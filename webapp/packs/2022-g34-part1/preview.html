Question #1 3 pts Answer: (A) 📐 2D Shapes, Symmetry & Paths + Counting & Sets

Buzz the Bee's Path

Buzz the bee starts in the top-left square of a 4×4 grid and wants to reach the flower in the bottom-right square. Which sequence of arrows gets him there?

Core Idea: Every valid path from top-left to bottom-right on a 4×4 grid needs exactly 3 steps right (→) and 3 steps down (↓).

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Draw a quick 4×4 grid on the whiteboard with 'B' in top-left and 'F' in bottom-right.
  2. Count how many jumps right it takes to go from column 1 to column 4: 3 jumps (not 4!).
  3. Count how many jumps down it takes to go from row 1 to row 4: 3 jumps.
  4. Check the arrow counts in each choice—only (A) → ↓ → ↓ ↓ → has 3 right arrows and 3 down arrows, then trace (A) square-by-square to confirm.

Ask the Circle

  • Does the order of right and down arrows change where Buzz finishes, as long as he stays inside the grid?
  • Why do we need 3 jumps instead of 4 on a 4-square-wide grid?

Common Traps

Kids who pick (C) or (D) only have 5 arrows total (missing one step down or right). Kids who count 4 squares instead of 3 jumps often get confused by (B) or (E), which have 4 down arrows and walk Buzz off the bottom of the board.

Try at Home

Draw a 4×4 grid on scrap paper with 'B' in top-left and 'F' in bottom-right, and let your child hop a coin step by step to see why 4 squares wide takes 3 jumps.

Question #2 3 pts Answer: (B) 📐 2D Shapes, Symmetry & Paths

Mirror Laser Maze

Laser beams bounce at 90° off slanted mirrors as shown in the example. Where does the laser beam exit in the second mirror box?

Core Idea: Both sides of every slanted mirror reflect light—slow down at the bottom-right mirror!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Sketch the 3×2 grid of mirrors (columns A, B, C; rows E on top, D on bottom) on the whiteboard.
  2. Trace with a red marker one mirror at a time: enter top-left going →, hit '\' so go ↓ to (A, D), hit '\' so go → to (B, D).
  3. At (B, D) hit '/' going ↑ to (B, E), hit '/' going → to (C, E), hit '\' going ↓ to (C, D).
  4. Pause at (C, D): the beam comes straight DOWN onto '/'. Which way does '/' tilt? Down-and-left! So the beam bounces LEFT (←) back to (B, D), hits the underside of '/', and goes straight DOWN out at B!

Ask the Circle

  • Imagine rolling a ball straight down onto a slide tilted like '/' — does the ball bounce to the left or to the right?
  • Can the same mirror bounce the laser twice—once on its top side and once on its bottom side?

Common Traps

Choice (D) is the #1 trap: students trace smoothly until (C, D) and carelessly bounce right out to D instead of bouncing left back toward column B. Choice (C) happens if they ignore the mirror at (C, D).

Try at Home

Sketch the 3×2 mirror box on paper and stand a pencil or pocket mirror along each diagonal slash so your child can bounce a finger beam off both sides.

Question #3 3 pts Answer: (C) 🧩 Deductive Logic & Constraints + 2D Shapes

Two Coins per Row & Column

Rossitza wants exactly 2 coins in every row and every column of the 5×5 grid. Which labeled coin must she move to an empty square?

Core Idea: Write the coin count next to every row and column—the crowded row and crowded column cross right on the coin to move!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Have students call out how many coins are in each row from top to bottom: 2, 2, 2, 1, 3. Circle Row 5 (too many: 3) and Row 4 (too few: 1).
  2. Now count each column from left to right: 3, 1, 2, 2, 2. Circle Column 1 (too many: 3) and Column 2 (too few: 1).
  3. Find where the 'too many' Row 5 and 'too many' Column 1 cross: bottom-left square = coin C!
  4. Move C into where Row 4 and Column 2 cross—now every single row and column has 2 coins.

Ask the Circle

  • Which row has 3 coins? Which column has 3 coins? Which single coin sits in BOTH of them at the same time?
  • Where is the exact empty square that coin C should move into?

Common Traps

Students who pick (B) see Column 1 has 3 coins and Row 4 is weird, so they grab B—moving B out of Row 4 would leave Row 4 with 0 coins! Students who pick (E) notice Row 5 has 3 coins without checking Column 3.

Try at Home

Lay out 10 pennies on a hand-drawn 5×5 grid and write the count beside each row and column—ask which coin sits at the crossing of a 3-coin row and a 3-coin column.

Question #4 3 pts Answer: (C) 🧩 Deductive Logic & Constraints + 3D Spatial

Opening the TRAIN Box

What is the smallest number of boxes Bill must move so he can open the lid of the dark TRAIN box at the bottom?

Core Idea: Work upwards from TRAIN to mark every box sitting on it—and don't count TRAIN itself!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Point to the dark TRAIN box at the bottom. Ask: which boxes sit directly on TRAIN's lid? 'stuffed animals' (left) and 'bedding' (right) — that's 2 boxes.
  2. Before we can lift 'stuffed animals', what sits on top of it? 'books' and 'sheet music' (+2 boxes).
  3. Before we can lift 'bedding', what else sits on top of it? 'sheet music' (already counted!) and 'board games' (+1 box).
  4. Check 'puzzles' and 'BOOKS' on the far right: 'board games' rests on 'puzzles', NOT the other way around, so once 'board games' is off, 'puzzles' stays put! Total = 2 + 2 + 1 = 5 boxes.

Ask the Circle

  • Do we need to move the 'puzzles' box to lift 'bedding', or is 'puzzles' underneath 'board games'?
  • Should we count the TRAIN box itself, or only the boxes blocking its lid?

Common Traps

Choice (B) 4 happens when a student misses that 'board games' overlaps the right corner of 'bedding' (or double-forgets 'books'). Choice (D) 6 happens when a student either counts the TRAIN box itself or unnecessarily moves 'puzzles'.

Try at Home

Stack books, board-game boxes, and cereal boxes on the table like the diagram so your child can test which top boxes must lift before the bottom lid opens.

Question #9 4 pts Answer: (B) 🔄 Patterns, Cycles & Step Simulation + Logic

Five Overtaking Cars

Cars 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 drive left (1 in front, 5 last). First the last car passes 2 cars; next the second-to-last car passes 2 cars; finally the middle car passes 2 cars. What is the final order?

Core Idea: Check the arrow direction first (left = front!), and rewrite all 5 numbers cleanly after every single pass.

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Point out the black arrow pointing LEFT: Car 1 is leading in front, Car 5 is last at the right! Write: 1 2 3 4 5.
  2. Move 1 — Last car (5) passes two cars ahead of it (4 and 3): write 1 2 5 3 4.
  3. Move 2 — Who is second-to-last NOW? Look at 1 2 5 3 4: last is 4, so second-to-last is 3! Car 3 passes 5 and 2: write 1 3 2 5 4.
  4. Move 3 — Who is the middle car NOW? In 1 3 2 5 4, the middle car is 2! Car 2 passes 3 and 1 to take the lead: 2 1 3 5 4 -> (B).

Ask the Circle

  • After Move 1, is Car 4 still the second-to-last car, or did someone else take that spot?
  • Bonus live demo: invite 5 volunteers to stand in a line facing left holding numbers 1–5 and act out the three passes!

Common Traps

Choice (C) 2, 1, 5, 3, 4 happens if students think 'second-to-last' means Car 4 (from the original order) instead of Car 3 in the updated line. Other errors come from treating right as 'ahead' instead of following the left arrow.

Try at Home

Line up 5 toy cars or numbered playing cards facing LEFT and physically act out each pass—calling out who is second-to-last and middle fresh after every single move.

Question #10 4 pts Answer: (C) ⚖️ Number Sense, Equations & Grids

Kangaroo Family Ages

Six kangaroos have ages 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 10. Four of their ages add up to 22. What are the ages of the other two kangaroos?

Core Idea: Instead of guessing four numbers that make 22, subtract 22 from the total age of ALL six kangaroos!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Add all six kangaroo ages together using friendly pairs: (2 + 8) + 10 + (4 + 6) + 5 = 10 + 10 + 10 + 5 = 35.
  2. If four kangaroos add up to 22, the remaining two kangaroos MUST add up to 35 − 22 = 13!
  3. Bonus parity shortcut: 13 is an ODD number, and 5 is the ONLY odd age in the family (2, 4, 6, 8, 10 are all even!), so 5 MUST be one of the two!
  4. 13 − 5 = 8, so the pair is 5 and 8 (C). Double-check the other four: 2 + 4 + 6 + 10 = 22.

Ask the Circle

  • Can two even numbers ever add up to 13? What does that immediately tell us about the 5-year-old kangaroo?
  • How can we add 2 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 8 + 10 in our heads super fast by making tens?

Common Traps

Students who guess-and-check 4 numbers often mis-add (e.g., thinking 4 + 5 + 6 + 8 = 22 when it's 23) or accidentally report two kangaroos from INSIDE the 22-group like 6 and 10 (E) or 2 and 8 (A).

Try at Home

Write 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 10 on small paper slips, group pairs into tens to find the total (35), and subtract 22 so your child sees how much faster total-minus-part is than guessing.

Question #11 4 pts Answer: (A) 🧩 Deductive Logic & Constraints

Five Vacation Postcards

Five postcards (A)–(E) were sent to Mike, Cara, Paula, Lexi, and Heather based on five clues. Which card did Mike get?

Core Idea: Start with the clues that match ONLY ONE picture—and look closely at the top-right corner of the ducks card!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Put (A) (B) (C) (D) (E) on the board and start with the slam-dunk clues: Lexi has a dog → (E); Heather has kangaroos → (B).
  2. Next check Paula: 'exactly two living creatures'. Between (A) [0 creatures], (C) [ladybug + fly = 2], and (D) [7 ducks], Paula must get (C)!
  3. Only (A) and (D) remain for Mike and Cara! Clue 1 says 'There are NO ducks on Mike's card' — (D) is full of ducks, so Mike MUST get (A)!
  4. Verify Cara's clue on (D): point to the bright yellow sun tucked into the top-right corner of the duck postcard (D)!

Ask the Circle

  • Why shouldn't we give (A) to Cara right away when we read 'Cara's card has the sun on it'?
  • How many cards actually have a sun in the picture?

Common Traps

This is a classic Kangaroo observation trap: kids read 'Cara's card has the sun on it', immediately cross out (A) for Cara without noticing the corner sun on (D), and then get stuck or guess (C).

Try at Home

Practice the 'lock in sure matches first' habit on clue puzzles—zoom in together on the small details in every picture before ruling out any card for the sun clue.

Question #12 4 pts Answer: (B) ⚖️ Number Sense, Equations & Grids

George's 3×3 Sum Mistake

George wanted every row and every column of his 3×3 number grid (9,1,5 / 3,7,6 / 4,7,4) to have the same sum, but made one mistake. Which number must he fix?

Core Idea: Changing ONE cell changes exactly ONE row sum and ONE column sum—find where the bad row and bad column cross!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Write the 3×3 grid on the board and add each row on the right: Row 1 = 9+1+5 = 15; Row 2 = 3+7+6 = 16; Row 3 = 4+7+4 = 15.
  2. Now add each column underneath: Col 1 = 9+3+4 = 16; Col 2 = 1+7+7 = 15; Col 3 = 5+6+4 = 15.
  3. The target sum is clearly 15! Both Row 2 (16) and Col 1 (16) are 1 too big.
  4. Circle the cell where Row 2 and Col 1 intersect: the number 3! Changing 3 → 2 fixes both sums to 15 at once -> (B).

Ask the Circle

  • How do we know the magic sum George wanted was 15 and not 16?
  • What number should George write in place of 3?

Common Traps

Students who only check rows see Row 2 (3, 7, 6) = 16 and might pick 'one of the 7s' (E) without checking Column 2 (1+7+7=15, which is already right!).

Try at Home

Copy the 3×3 numbers onto scrap paper, write every row sum to the right and every column sum underneath, and circle where the one wrong row and one wrong column cross.

Question #17 5 pts Answer: (B) 🔢 Systematic Counting & Sets

Wanda's Shapes

From 6 shapes (small white square, large red square, small red triangle, large white triangle, large white circle, small red circle), Wanda picked a group containing 2 colored, 2 large, and 2 round shapes. What is the smallest number of shapes she could have picked?

Core Idea: One single shape can count toward MULTIPLE rules at once—start with the round shapes because there are only two!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Draw all 6 shapes on the board. Ask: how many ROUND shapes exist in the whole list? Only TWO: the large white circle and the small red circle!
  2. So Wanda MUST pick both circles. Check our scoreboard with those 2 circles: Round = 2 (done!), Colored = 1 (small red circle), Large = 1 (large white circle).
  3. We still need 1 more COLORED shape and 1 more LARGE shape (without adding any round shapes). Can ONE shape do both jobs?
  4. Yes! Add the LARGE RED SQUARE: now we have 2 colored, 2 large, and 2 round using just 3 shapes total -> (B).

Ask the Circle

  • Why couldn't Wanda finish with just 2 shapes?
  • Why was starting with '2 round shapes' easier than starting with '2 colored shapes'?

Common Traps

Choice (E) 6 comes from thinking 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 without realizing one shape can be both red AND large! Choice (C) 4 happens if kids pick separate shapes (e.g., small red triangle + large white triangle) instead of combining both traits in the large red square.

Try at Home

Cut 6 quick paper shapes (or use toy blocks of two sizes and colors) and ask your child to build a 2-round, 2-large, 2-colored set using the fewest pieces by picking double-duty shapes.

Question #18 5 pts Answer: (D) 🔢 Systematic Counting & Sets

Three-Team Football Tournament

Three football teams each play the other two teams once (win = 3 pts, tie = 1 pt, loss = 0 pts). Which point total is IMPOSSIBLE for a team?

Core Idea: Focus on just ONE team: with 3 teams in the tournament, each team plays only 2 games!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Ask the kids: if Team Kangaroo is in a 3-team tournament (A, B, C) and plays each other team once, how many games does Team Kangaroo play? Just 2 games! (Don't confuse 3 total games in the tournament with 2 games per team!)
  2. In each game, a team can score 0 (loss), 1 (tie), or 3 (win) points.
  3. List every way to add two scores from {0, 1, 3}: 0+0=0, 0+1=1, 1+1=2, 0+3=3, 1+3=4, 3+3=6.
  4. Every number in the choices shows up EXCEPT 5 -> (D)!

Ask the Circle

  • How many games are played in the whole tournament vs. how many games ONE team plays?
  • Is there any way to get 5 points in two games when a win is 3 and a tie is 1?

Common Traps

The biggest 3rd-grade trap is thinking '3 teams means each team plays 3 games!' If a team played 3 games, 3 + 1 + 1 = 5 would look possible, leading kids to guess (A) 1 or (B) 2 instead.

Try at Home

Draw a triangle for Teams A, B, and C with lines connecting each pair, then count how many lines touch Team A so your child sees clearly why each team plays 2 games, not 3.

Question #19 5 pts Answer: (E) 🧊 3D Spatial & Cubes + Measurement

Ant on the Cube Pyramid

A 3-layer pyramid is built from 10 cm cubes. An ant climbs up and over the pyramid along the red line. How long is the red path?

Core Idea: Split the ant's walk into three separate directions: all the UP steps, all the FLAT steps ('view from above'), and all the DOWN steps!

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Ask: why did the test give us 'view from above'? Look down from the ceiling—all the flat horizontal segments line up into ONE straight red line across 3 cubes = 3 × 10 cm = 30 cm! (No need to guess the 5 cm ledges, though 5+5+10+5+5 = 30 cm too!)
  2. Now count all the vertical UP climbs on the left side: the ant climbs 3 cube layers high = 3 × 10 cm = 30 cm up.
  3. Now count all the vertical DOWN climbs on the right side (visible on the right walls): 3 cube layers down = 3 × 10 cm = 30 cm down.
  4. Total length = 30 cm (up) + 30 cm (flat across) + 30 cm (down) = 90 cm -> (E).

Ask the Circle

  • If an elevator goes up 3 floors of 10 cm, does it matter that it stopped on ledges in between? How high did it climb in total?
  • Why can't we see the three vertical red lines on the left side of the 3D drawing, even though the ant climbed up there?

Common Traps

Choice (B) 60 cm is a huge trap: kids see the 3 vertical red lines on the right (30 cm) and the flat segments (30 cm), forgetting the 3 hidden vertical walls the ant climbed UP on the left side! Choice (C) 70 cm comes from miscounting the half-cube 5 cm ledges as 10 cm.

Try at Home

Stack 6 wooden blocks or sugar cubes into a 3-layer staircase and trace a piece of yarn up, across, and down—counting total up-steps, total flat length, and total down-steps separately.

Question #20 5 pts Answer: (E) 📐 2D Shapes, Symmetry & Paths

Alma's Hexagonal Road Tile

Alma places one rotatable hexagon road tile in the center so a child at house A can travel to B and E, but NOT to D. Which two tiles work?

Core Idea: Count steps around the hexagon from A: B is right next door (+1), D is straight across (+3, forbidden!), and E is +4.

Whiteboard Walkthrough

  1. Draw the 6-sided hub on the board and circle what we MUST touch: A, B (next to A), and E (skipping F on one side, skipping C & D on the other), while keeping D empty/disconnected!
  2. Eliminate fast! Tile 3 only touches every-other side, so it can NEVER connect neighbors A and B. Tile 2 only has 3 adjacent roads next to each other—too bunched up to reach both B and E!
  3. Eliminate Tile 4: every road on Tile 4 goes straight across to the opposite side! Since D is directly opposite A, ANY road touching A on Tile 4 goes straight to D!
  4. That leaves Tile 1 and Tile 5 -> (E)! Verify both by rotating: Tile 1 connects (E, F, A, B) skipping C and D; Tile 5 rotated connects (A, B, C) plus E while skipping D and F!

Ask the Circle

  • Look at Tile 4—what do you notice about where every single road on Tile 4 goes? If you enter at A, where do you end up?
  • Why can't Tile 3 ever connect house A to house B no matter how much we spin it?

Common Traps

Students often pick (C) 1 and 4 or (D) 4 and 5 because Tile 4 has 4 roads and looks flexible at a glance, missing that opposite-side symmetry forces A to connect directly to opposite house D!

Try at Home

Cut out a quick paper hexagon with edges labeled A–F and draw the tile roads on small hexagon cutouts that your child can physically spin on the table to test paths to B, E, and D.
